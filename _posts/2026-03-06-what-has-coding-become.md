## What Has Coding Become? A CS PhD Student’s Perspective on Modern Programming

I write code every day, and I hope I will continue doing so in the future. However, the world is changing rapidly. AI is already better at coding than I am in many ways. This piece is a reflection on my experience with coding in the age of AI.

I first learned about coding through C in middle school. At the time, it mostly involved playing around with the Windows command prompt and trolling my classmates. I remember creating an executable titled “DO NOT CLICK ON ME” that kept opening CMD windows until the computer slowed to a crawl. Watching the machine spiral into chaos felt like magic.

Later, I went to college as a Computer Science major. I took courses on data structures and algorithms, but learning them without any intuition about their real-world applications made the subject feel abstract and uninteresting. Eventually, I switched my major to Physics and I was able to find meaning in what I study.

Ironically, physics brought me back to programming. During an internship, I had the opportunity to write software related to satellite systems. For the first time, I was writing code that interacted with real systems and real data. That experience rekindled my curiosity about computers.

Up to that point, coding had always felt like a difficult skill to master. The common advice was simple: read the documentation whenever you get stuck. Today, however, the workflow has changed. Instead of searching through documentation, many of us simply ask AI.

I still believe that official documentation should remain the primary source of truth. But in practice, it often feels like it has become a secondary resource.

## Agentic Programming

Despite all the hype, the world has not ended yet. AI is not fully reliable for coding. It is extremely powerful, but it performs best when guided carefully by its human operator.

When discussing agentic programming, I like to point people to [this article]( https://antirez.com/news/154 ) by *Salvatore Sanfilippo*, the creator of **Redis**

Here is the gist of it.

Vibe-coding is not the way. Without clear instructions and well-defined specifications, we should not let AI write code—at least not unsupervised. There are two major reasons for this.

First, AI works best when it is given structured guidance and close supervision.

Second, if you rely too heavily on AI without understanding what it produces, you will eventually reach a point where your own system surpasses your comprehension. When bugs inevitably appear, you will not have the knowledge required to fix them—let alone optimize the system.

## Should I Still Code?

Yes.

You may spend less time typing code yourself, but you still need to think at a very detailed level. The fundamental requirement has not changed: you must fully understand the systems you are building, even if AI helps write parts of them.

In many ways, AI has simply shifted our time allocation.

It gives us more time to think, and less time to type.

Personally, whenever I write code now, I remind myself that AI has bought us more time to think. And I want my thinking to drive my doing.

## "English is the best programming language."

> Jensen Huang once said:
>
> **“English is the best programming language.”**

The idea behind this statement is that programming is becoming increasingly accessible. If you can clearly express an idea, the technical barrier to implementing that idea is shrinking.

In that sense, the role of the programmer is evolving. We are no longer just people who write syntax correctly. Instead, we are people who **think clearly, design systems, and communicate precise intentions**.

AI may generate the code.

But the thinking still belongs to us.
